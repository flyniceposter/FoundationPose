1. What does “6D pose”
mean?
Three numbers describe position; three independent
quantities describe orientation.


Position: where is it?
Imagine putting a mug on a table. Move it left/right, up/down
and nearer/farther from the camera. Those three directions
describe translation, usually written as t = [tx, ty, tz].

Orientation: which way does it face?
Now leave the mug in the same place and turn or tilt it.
Orientation has three rotational degrees of freedom. A
rotation matrix stores this orientation without being just a list
of three angles.

Why six?
Three positional degrees of freedom plus three rotational
degrees of freedom make six. “6D” does not mean six spatial
dimensions. A 3-by-3 rotation matrix contains nine entries,
but its geometric constraints leave three independent
rotational degrees of freedom.

2. What goes into your
experiment?
The current run is model-based RGB-D pose estimation.


RGB image
A normal color picture: what the scene looks like. RGB stands
for red, green and blue.

Depth image
A depth value at each pixel estimates the distance along the
camera viewing axis. Together with camera calibration, depth
helps convert image pixels into 3D points. Invalid or noisy
depth can make the estimate worse.

Visible-object mask
A binary image marks the pixels belonging to the visible part
of the object. Your baseline receives ground-truth visible
masks. It is not testing whether a separate detector can find
the object.

CAD mesh and calibration
The mesh is the object’s 3D shape. Camera intrinsics, usually
called K, connect 3D camera coordinates to image pixels.
Your run uses the provided CAD models, not reconstructed
models.

Ground truth: the reference answer
BOP supplies the reference pose for comparison. Its masks
are deliberately used as inputs here. The reference pose is
used for evaluation; the inspected ranking-error helper
returns a placeholder and does not use it to pick the winning
pose.

Training is different from inference
Training learns network weights from examples. Inference
applies the already-trained networks. Your server is running
inference and evaluation, not training new FoundationPose
weights.

3. How does FoundationPose
find a pose?
Think: propose placements, improve them, then choose one.


Step 1 — Prepare the observation
Load the RGB image, depth image and object mask. The
mask and depth provide an initial estimate of the object’s
position.

Step 2 — Propose candidate orientations
Place the model in many plausible orientations. The original
log showed 252 initial candidates; symmetry clustering can
change this count for different objects.

Step 3 — Refine each candidate
Render the model and compare the rendering with the
observed scene. A learned refinement network adjusts
candidate poses. Your registration method defaults to five
refinement iterations.

Step 4 — Score and select
A learned scoring network ranks the refined candidates. The
highest-ranked candidate becomes the saved pose. This
score is a network preference, not a ground-truth accuracy
measurement.

Registration versus tracking
Registration starts by finding a pose from candidates.
Tracking uses a previous pose to help process a later frame.
This LINEMOD runner registers each image independently. A
successful video demo is not the same experimental protocol
as this dataset evaluation.

   + mask + CAD                                 poses                               placements   save winner

4. Understanding the saved
pose matrix
A pose moves points from the CAD model’s coordinates into
the camera’s coordinates.


The rule
For a model point, first rotate it using R, then shift it using t.
The model and camera have different coordinate origins. A
4-by-4 homogeneous matrix stores the complete transform in
one array.

A simple example
Assume no rotation. Let a model point be [0.01, 0, 0] metres
and the object translation be [0.10, -0.02, 0.80] metres. Its
camera point is [0.11, -0.02, 0.80] metres. This is an
illustrative example, not a measured pose from your run.

The unit conversion that matters
Your estimator works with metres. BOP mesh vertices and
ground-truth translations are provided in millimetres. Analysis
multiplies predicted translation by 1,000 before comparing
them. Thus 0.80 m equals 800 mm.

A common mistake to avoid
Do not invert this matrix or apply a second mesh-centering
correction. The inspected estimator already returns a
transform for the original CAD model. Adding another
correction would create a false error.




       pcamera = Rpmodel + t

       Saved 4 × 4 layout:                          [ R (3 × 3) | t (3 × 1) ]
                                                                [    0    0                  0   |   1        ]

7. What do the error metrics
measure?
Smaller errors are better. Recall is different: larger is better.


Translation error — millimetres
Distance between the predicted and reference translation
vectors. Example: 4 mm means the model origin is displaced
by about 4 mm; it does not mean every surface point is only
4 mm away.

Rotation error — degrees
The angular difference between the two orientations. Zero
degrees means identical orientation. Your raw rotation
statistic is not corrected for object symmetry.

ADD — millimetres
Transform each CAD vertex with the predicted pose and the
same vertex with the reference pose. ADD averages their
distances. It captures the combined effect of position and
orientation.

ADI / ADD-S — millimetres
Instead of requiring the same vertex, use a nearest-neighbor
vertex. Your audited implementation queries reference
vertices against the predicted point cloud. The direction
matters. This makes some symmetric alignments less harshly
penalized.

MSSD — millimetres
For each annotated symmetry, compute the largest
corresponding-vertex surface distance; choose the smallest
result across those symmetries. It is sensitive to the worst
surface displacement.

MSPD — pixels
Perform the analogous symmetry-aware maximum
comparison after projecting model vertices into the image. It
measures image-space alignment rather than 3D distance.




Metric definitions: official BOP toolkit [3]. Local scripts implement diagnostic measurements, not official scoring.

8. Worked examples: errors
and symmetry
These numbers and drawings are teaching examples, not
your experimental results.


Average versus maximum
Suppose corresponding transformed vertices are 2, 3 and 4
mm apart. Their average is 3 mm, while their maximum is 4
mm. ADD emphasizes the average; MSSD uses a maximum
before choosing among annotated symmetries.

What “ADD(-S) < 0.1 diameter ” means
For a toy object with diameter 100 mm, the threshold is 10
mm. An error of 7 mm passes; an error of 12 mm fails. An
error exactly equal to 10 mm fails because your comparison
uses strict less-than.

Why rotation alone can be misleading
A perfectly untextured rectangular block can look identical
after a 180-degree turn about an appropriate axis. Its raw
rotation difference can be 180 degrees even though a
symmetry-aware comparison finds the placement equivalent.

How your analysis handles this
The conventional LINEMOD ADD(-S) diagnostic uses ADI for
eggbox (10) and glue (11), and ADD for the other included
objects. MSSD and MSPD instead use the discrete
transformations in BOP metadata, including their translation
offsets. Free nearest neighbors are not the same thing as a
globally consistent object symmetry.

9. Recall is not “percent
correct coordinates”
A threshold turns each pose error into a pass or fail.


Pose recall
If 90 out of 100 expected poses have errors below the chosen
threshold, recall is 90%. That is a fraction of object instances
passing a test, not a percentage of matrix entries that are
correct.

Missing predictions count
If 10 expected poses are missing, the denominator is still 100.
Reporting only the 90 surviving outputs could hide failures.
Your full expected-set recall counts missing and invalid
predictions as failures.

Pooled versus object macro
Pooled recall combines every expected pose, so objects with
more images get more weight. Object-macro recall first
computes recall for each object and then averages the object
recalls equally.

A diagnostic is not an official benchmark score
Your ADD(-S) recall, MSSD < 0.1 diameter recall and MSPD <
5 pixel recall are diagnostic measurements on this chosen
split. Official BOP19 localization AR averages recalls over
protocol-defined thresholds for VSD, MSSD and MSPD, then
combines them. VSD concerns visible rendered surfaces.

What an official claim would require
Use the appropriate official protocol and toolkit, target list,
matching and symmetry conventions, model sampling and
required inputs. Disclose GT-mask input and any recovered
rounded predictions. This workflow has not computed VSD or
official BOP AR, and no official benchmark accuracy is
claimed.




Protocol distinction: BOP localization task description [2].

12. Reading the graphs
without getting misled
Always read units, denominators and missing counts.


Error distributions
The plot below groups translation and raw rotation errors by
object. The box shows the middle half of valid errors; its
center line is the median. Whiskers summarize spread under
the plotting convention. Outlier points were hidden to keep
the scale readable.

What a low mean can hide
A few very large errors can matter even when typical
performance is good. Read the median and 90th percentile
as well as the mean. Hidden outlier markers do not mean the
outliers disappeared from the CSV.

Why some groups are empty
This plot is from recovered data. Later objects have no valid
recovered predictions. An empty group is missing data, not a
perfect zero-error result.

The other plots
Recall bars compare per-object passing fractions. A
recall-versus-threshold curve shows how strictness changes
the score. Visibility/distance strata describe associations;
different object mixtures can confound them.

13. What should the next
experiment test?
A useful experiment changes one factor and measures the
difference.


Question and hypothesis
Because the baseline receives ideal GT masks, test how pose
quality changes when those masks are imperfect. Hypothesis:
small mask boundary errors or image shifts reduce pose
recall, especially when little of the object is visible.

Controlled procedure
Keep RGB, depth, CAD model, calibration and pose settings
fixed. Compare the original mask with predefined erosion,
dilation and shift variants. Write variants to a new directory
instead of editing the dataset. Use the same frames and
paired seeds for every condition.

Development versus held-out data
The proposal uses 20 development and 20 held-out frames
per object, selected deterministically. Tune any mitigation on
development frames only. Since the full baseline has already
been inspected, this is a study split, not an untouched official
benchmark test.

What to measure
Record mask IoU, paired changes in ADD(-S), MSSD and
MSPD recall, translation/rotation percentiles and runtime. A
useful result explains a reproducible relationship, rather than
only showing a higher number.

What has not happened yet
No mask-noise, depth-noise or refinement-ablation GPU study
has been launched. The full proposed mask study needs
20,280 registrations and requires a separate approval. The
current approval covers the 10,614-frame baseline
continuation only.

15. A short glossary and
self-check
If you can answer the questions below, you understand the
workflow.


Vocabulary
Pose: position and orientation. Inference: applying trained
weights. Ground truth: the reference answer. Mesh: a 3D model
made of vertices and faces. Intrinsics: camera calibration. Mask:
selected object pixels. Symmetry: an equivalent object
transformation. Checkpoint: saved completed work. Baseline: a
fixed reference experiment. Ablation: a controlled change to test
one factor.

Check your understanding
1. Why can translation error be small while surface error is large?
2. Why is a 180-degree rotation not always a physical failure?
3. Why is 99.75% on the recovered prefix not final full-run
accuracy?
4. What separates a running process from a successfully
completed evaluation?
5. Why does changing both masks and depth at once make
conclusions harder?

Answers
1. Wrong orientation can move surface points even when the
model origin is close.
2. A valid object symmetry can make the placements equivalent.
3. The prefix excludes most later objects and comes from
rounded logs.
4. Normal completion plus validated saved outputs and
coverage, followed by analysis checks.
5. You cannot identify which change caused the effect.

One sentence you can tell your advisor
“I am evaluating pretrained FoundationPose on a 13-object BOP
LINEMOD RGB-D split with ground-truth visible masks. The
recovered baseline uses a disclosed rounded-log prefix,
native-precision continuation, and diagnostic pose metrics;
official BOP scores have not been computed.”
